// Selected Work

Becoming an Identity Provider

Toddle · 2026

Like nearly every SaaS product, Toddle sat on one side of a login: the side that asks. We took assertions from Google, Microsoft, Okta, whatever a district happened to run, and that was enough as long as people only needed to get into Toddle. It stopped being enough once the traffic reversed — partner tools and our own internal applications needed to authenticate people against Toddle. Asking someone else who a user is turns out to be a very different problem from being the one who answers.

I built the SAML half of that: Toddle as an identity provider, issuing signed assertions to third-party service providers, with the metadata exchange, certificate handling, and per-relying-party configuration that comes with it. Sign-on starts at Toddle: the user authenticates with us, picks the application, and we confirm they actually belong to the organisation that relying party is configured for before anything gets signed — so what Toddle is willing to assert comes out of the trust configuration rather than out of whoever happens to be asking. For the OIDC half I did the planning and scoping, wrote the design spec — authorization-code flow, silent SSO, account linking — and I’m guiding the implementation as it lands.

The interesting part wasn’t the protocol work. As a service provider you can lean on someone else’s judgment about who a user is; as an identity provider, everyone downstream leans on yours. Most of the hard decisions came out of that shift — from consuming trust to issuing it.

Impact — Toddle now sits on both sides of the federation boundary — consuming external IdPs for school sign-in, and issuing identity to third-party and internal applications. SAML is shipped; OIDC is being built against the spec I wrote.

What I’d redo — The scope of the first cut. I built the SAML side against a deadline and left some capability out to make it. Nothing load-bearing, and I’d make the same trade again — but a couple of those pieces belong in the initial design rather than in the follow-up queue, and I’d argue harder for them up front next time.

Self-serve SSO, From Scratch

Toddle · 2023–2024

When I picked this up, SSO at Toddle covered the two providers that mattered most at the time: Google and Microsoft OAuth. That had been the right call for years, but the ground had shifted — schools were increasingly arriving on Okta, Azure AD over SAML, ADFS, or a district’s in-house IdP, and each of those was still a bespoke engineering project. On top of that, every school shared one global login page, so as the provider list grew the page got crowded, and enabling an already-supported provider for a school still required an engineer — usually a flag flip in the database, sometimes a small backend change alongside it. Individually trivial, but it put a ticket, a review, and a deploy on the critical path of a school going live.

I built the company’s first generic SAML and OAuth integrations, working against any standards-compliant provider, plus a self-serve login page customisation system: per-org branding, per-org auth methods, configured by admins without engineering involvement. Method-level allowlisting is enforced server-side, so disabling a method for a school actually disables it — including on the global login URL. The global page still works for orgs that prefer it.

Impact — Moved Toddle from two hardcoded providers to any standards-compliant SAML or OAuth IdP, and eliminated a manual-ticket workflow that was consuming ~15 engineer-hours/month across 500+ organisations. SSO enablement went from a ticket queue measured in hours to a toggle measured in seconds — and identity integration stopped being a procurement blocker for enterprise deals.

What I’d redo — Two things. The SAML library: it works, but its abstractions leak and the docs are thin — the alternatives at the time weren’t better, which doesn’t make me happier about the dependency. And the admin setup UX: an admin who has configured SSO before will follow it fine, but a first-timer without docs has to think harder than they should. Information design over engineering on that pass.

Workflow Orchestration on Temporal

Toddle · 2024–2026

Long-running work — data syncs, bulk exports, migrations — ran on a homegrown architecture that predated the scale we were putting through it. It was hard to observe and harder to recover: retries were ad hoc, partial failures could leave inconsistent state, and each team that needed a background job ended up building its own scaffolding around the same set of problems.

I designed and built a workflow orchestration framework on Temporal with progress tracking, automatic retries, and graceful degradation built in, so teams get durability without hand-rolling it. It’s since been adopted by multiple teams to run long-running background jobs reliably across regions.

Impact — Became the standard path for long-running work at Toddle, adopted by multiple teams across regions — replacing per-team scaffolding with one durable, observable framework.

What I’d redo — The abstraction leans toward flexibility over opinion. Teams can express almost any workflow shape, which also means the framework can’t always tell them when they’ve expressed one badly. I’d trade some of that generality for guardrails.

Self-serve Client Onboarding

Toddle · 2022–2023

Onboarding a school meant migrating student, staff, parent, and class data out of their SIS — PowerSchool, Clever, Veracross, iSAMS, or something in-house — and into Toddle. Previously that meant an engineer running database updates by hand, usually across several rounds of back-and-forth to fix data-shape issues. That was workable at the volumes we’d been onboarding, but it was slow for the school, expensive for engineering, and a ceiling on how fast we could grow.

I worked on a self-serve rostering module: schools configure their own migration, monitor its progress, control the related settings, and opt into automatic recurring sync. PRD, scoping, UX, and implementation were all in scope. I also optimised the cron scheduling behind it to cut AWS Lambda invocations during peak hours.

Impact — Removed developer-in-the-loop from the rostering happy path for a network of 200+ schools. Engineering now only gets pulled in for genuinely unusual source data.

What I’d redo — Honestly, less than I’d like — later design iterations haven’t found a meaningfully simpler shape. That’s either a good sign or a sign I should look harder.

Also at Toddle: an async document-generation platform on AWS (Lambda, SQS, S3) that replaced timeout-prone synchronous downloads; a Slack-integrated release approval system that now gates every production release; MFA; a passwordless parent signup flow with child-scoped invite tokens; and a lot of production incident response.
